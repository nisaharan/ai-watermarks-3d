Few buildings anywhere are recognised as widely. Standing on the edge of Sydney Harbour in New South Wales, Australia, and serving as the country's foremost venue for the performing arts, the Sydney Opera House owes that recognition above all to one thing: a shell-shaped roof that has come to stand for the city and for the nation. Millions come each year to look at it.
The Danish architect Jørn Utzon won the international competition to design it in 1957, and construction began two years after that. It ran more than a decade. Delays accumulated, costs climbed, and long before the work was finished Utzon had resigned from the project he had won. Queen Elizabeth II opened the building in 1973. For Australian culture the date was a landmark.
Inside are the Concert Hall, the Joan Sutherland Theatre, the Drama Theatre and a range of other performance spaces, which between them stage thousands of performances a year, from opera and classical music to theatre and contemporary work. Several resident companies keep that programme rich and varied. The building is central to cultural life in Australia.
As architecture it is generally counted a masterpiece of modern design. The interlocking shells of the roof were an ambitious engineering problem for their time. Solving it required innovative methods, and those methods went on to influence how buildings were constructed elsewhere in the world. Architects and engineers still study them.
UNESCO added the site to the World Heritage List in 2007, citing its outstanding value. It is a testament to creative ambition and to technical achievement. Visitors arrive from all over the world, and the halls remain in active use. For Australians the building has become a lasting symbol of identity and pride.
