On the edge of Sydney Harbour, in New South Wales, Australia, stands a building almost everyone recognises. The Sydney Opera House is the country's leading centre for the performing arts, and its shell-shaped roof has come to stand for the city and for the nation alike. Millions arrive every year simply to admire the design.
It began with a competition. A Danish architect, Jørn Utzon, won it in 1957. Ground was broken in 1959, and the work then ran for more than a decade: the delays were significant, the costs rose, and Utzon resigned before the building was complete. Queen Elizabeth II opened it in 1973, a major milestone in Australian cultural history.
Inside is a wide range of performance spaces — the Concert Hall, the Joan Sutherland Theatre, the Drama Theatre. Thousands of performances pass through them each year, from opera and classical music to theatre and contemporary entertainment. Several companies are resident, and their work makes for a rich and varied artistic programme. Few buildings play so vital a role in a nation's cultural life.
Architecturally it is widely regarded as a masterpiece of modern design. The interlocking shells that make up the roof were an ambitious engineering challenge for their time, and bringing the original vision to life demanded innovative solutions; those methods went on to influence construction techniques around the world. Architects and engineers still study it.
UNESCO added the site to its World Heritage List in 2007, citing outstanding value. It is a testament to creative ambition and technical achievement both. Visitors come from every corner of the globe, the halls remain in active use, and the building has become a lasting symbol of Australian identity and cultural pride.
